## Instagram caption (draft, not posted)

Before 1925, if you wanted sound to be louder, you needed a bigger horn. Early phonographs used no electricity at all: a needle, a diaphragm, a horn. By 1922 a park in California had a horn 35 feet long.

Then, at General Electric in Schenectady, Chester Rice and Edward Kellogg tried something plainer: a coil of wire, a magnet and a paper cone. No horn at all.

The odd part is that the idea wasn't new. Ernst Siemens had described a coil moving in a magnet's field in 1874. What Rice and Kellogg worked out was how to make a simple cone sound right, and an amplifier strong enough to drive it. RCA sold it in 1926 as the Radiola 104, amplifier built in.

Then everything around the speaker changed: stereo (Alan Blumlein's patent, filed 14 December 1931), the Walkman (1 July 1979), the iPod (2001), wireless earbuds. Open almost any of them up and you'll find the same three things: a coil, a magnet, a cone pushing air.

Wording choices made deliberately:
- **"Most", not "almost every".** Other driver types are in real use: balanced-armature drivers in many in-ear monitors and hearing aids, piezo buzzers, electrostatic and planar speakers. No reliable market-share figure was found. The "over 99 %" figure in circulation comes from a Substack post and was not used.
- **"Tried something plainer", not "invented the moving coil".** The coil-in-a-magnet idea goes back to Siemens (1874) and Lodge (British patent 9712, 1898). Jensen and Pridham (Magnavox) also had moving-coil drivers in 1915, but they fed horns. Rice and Kellogg's contribution was the hornless direct-radiator cone in a baffle, together with an amplifier to drive it. This is the twist in beat 8.
- **"Built in" for the Radiola 104.** Radiomuseum lists it as speaker plus integrated amplifier. The price is left out because sources disagree ($250 or $275).
- **No Blumlein cinema anecdote.** The story that he got annoyed at a talkie's single speaker comes from his family, with differing dates, so it was left out.
- **Beat 3 says "early phonographs", not Edison's own machine with a horn.** The 1878 tinfoil phonograph in the photo predates the big-horn era, so the image is labelled with Edison and 1878, and the narration speaks of the acoustic era in general.
