## Instagram caption (draft, not posted)

On 24 October 1946, a V-2 rocket rose straight up from White Sands, New Mexico.

It was a German missile, assembled after the war from captured parts. Where the warhead used to be, engineers had put instruments and a 35 mm movie camera. The camera took one frame every second and a half.

The rocket reached about 65 miles (105 km), just past the 100 km line we now call the edge of space. The highest photo before that came from a balloon, 13.7 miles up.

Then it fell. The camera was smashed in the desert. The film, sealed in a steel cassette, was fine. Fred Rulli, a 19-year-old soldier on the recovery team, remembered the scientists "jumping up and down like kids."

In 1950, Clyde Holliday, the APL engineer behind the camera, wrote in National Geographic that the pictures showed "how our Earth would look to visitors from another planet coming in on a space ship."

Less than two years earlier, the same kind of rocket had been falling on London.
