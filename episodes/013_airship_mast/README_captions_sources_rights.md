## Instagram caption (draft, not posted)

In December 1929, Al Smith, the former New York governor fronting the Empire State Building, announced that its top would be a mooring mast for airships. Passengers would step off a zeppelin and down a gangplank, nearly a quarter of a mile above Fifth Avenue.

The mast added about two hundred feet and lifted the tower to 1,250 feet, comfortably above the Chrysler Building's 1,046. Some historians think the height was the real point.

Then there was the wind. Updrafts race up the walls of a tall building, and an airship tied only by its nose has no ground crew to hold its tail. In 1927 a gust stood the Navy airship Los Angeles almost on end at a mast in New Jersey.

In September 1931 a small, privately owned airship touched the mast for about three minutes in strong wind, the New York Times reported. On 30 September the Goodyear blimp Columbia tried to collect two bags of mail; after a dozen passes, the rope tore out of a worker's hands. The only delivery, reports say, was a bundle of newspapers lowered on a rope.

But the mast found another job. On 22 December 1931 NBC began experimental television broadcasts from the building. A TV antenna went up on top in 1950, and New York radio and TV stations still broadcast from it today.

Would you have walked that gangplank? 👇

**Cut or softened on purpose:**
- **Which airship made the three-minute contact:** Smithsonian/NYT say a privately owned dirigible (pilot named as Lt. William McCraken); Wikipedia's "1931 in aviation" says the Navy blimp J-4. The narration says only "a small airship".
- **Order of the September 1931 events:** sources disagree on whether the newspaper drop came two weeks before or after the mail attempt, so the narration gives no sequence and hedges the newspapers ("reports say").
- **The staged mail-bag footage:** one summary of the LoC caption says a cameraman later carried a rope up so the bags could be filmed being hauled aboard. Not confirmed verbatim, so left out.
- **Hydrogen/helium risk:** no solid source tying it to the ESB decision; cut.
- **Al Smith quotes ("swing in the breeze… gangplank", "economic investment rather than spectacular standards"):** not confirmed verbatim against the NYT original, so paraphrased only.
- **Armstrong's FM tests from the ESB (1934–35)** and the 85th-floor RCA lease: true and sourced, but cut for length.
- **"Docking platform on the 102nd floor":** shown only as a label in the height schematic, not narrated.
- **The Met's 1930 "dirigible docked" composite:** not used.
